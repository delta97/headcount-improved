# Executive position paper

## Why these two templates

Both are proven in use: the position paper and the framework recommendation each turned live
executive pushback into an approved structure within days. They work because they concede the
objection before proposing past it — the reader finds their own concern stated, sharper than
they said it, before the ask.

## Template A — the position paper

For "we should do X" arguments. Prose-heavy, five sections, in order:

1. **Purpose** — one paragraph: what this document proposes and who decides.
2. **The problem today** — concrete and costed, in the reader's terms, including the strongest
   version of the objections already raised. Name who raised what; metabolizing pushback is
   the move, not avoiding it.
3. **Proposal** — what to do, deliberately scoped small. State the thesis in one memorable
   line if it has one ("engineering builds the road once; users drive on it themselves").
4. **Explicit non-goals** — what this does *not* attempt. This section does more to win
   approval than the proposal: it is where scope fear goes to die. Phase boundaries live here.
5. **What we need to prove, and the immediate next step** — the falsifiable claims the pilot
   tests, and one concrete action with an owner and a date.

## Template B — the framework recommendation

For "how should this work" designs (intake processes, governance, operating models). Numbered
sections:

1. Context and the forcing question.
2. **Options compared** — a table of three real options, each with its failure mode stated as
   plainly as its benefit. A comparison without failure modes is advertising.
3. **Recommended framework** — the staged workflow (Stage 0 → N), each stage with entry and
   exit criteria.
4. **Scoring rubric** where prioritization is involved — criteria, weights summing to a round
   total, and the tie-break rule.
5. **Operating cadence** — who meets, how often, deciding what.
6. **Pilot plan** — week-by-week for the first cycle, with the review point where the
   framework itself gets judged.

## Method

1. Gather the actual objections from the meeting notes that prompted this — quote positions
   accurately and attribute them; a strawman here costs the whole document its credibility.
2. Pick the template by the ask: a decision wants A, a design wants B. A design smuggled into
   a decision document reads as overreach.
3. Draft, then cut a third. These documents win by being shorter than the reader feared.
4. End with the decision requested, the decider, and the date it is needed by.
